Dr Isa El-Buba Foundation (DISEF)
Departmental Training Module
Fish Farming

1. Module Title
Fish Farming

2. Departmental Vision
To raise disciplined, knowledgeable, hardworking and God-fearing trainees who can establish, manage and support profitable, hygienic and sustainable fish farming enterprises for food security, employment creation, community development and Kingdom impact.

3. Departmental Mission
The Fish Farming Department exists to train young people in practical aquaculture skills, including pond preparation, fish stocking, feeding, water quality management, fish health, record keeping, harvesting, marketing, financial planning and responsible farm management.
The department will prepare trainees to become fish farm assistants, pond managers, aquaculture entrepreneurs, community trainers, cooperative members, farm supervisors and responsible stewards of God’s creation.

4. Programme Philosophy
Fish farming is both a skill and a responsibility. It requires patience, observation, discipline, cleanliness, accurate feeding, proper water management, good records and financial wisdom.
Fish cannot speak like human beings, but they respond to poor management. Bad water, overfeeding, underfeeding, poor stocking, overcrowding, disease, careless handling and dirty farm practices can lead to poor growth, losses and death.
Therefore, this department must train trainees to be observant, honest, consistent and diligent. A good fish farmer does not guess everything. A good fish farmer watches, records, learns, corrects and improves.
Fish farming also teaches Kingdom lessons. It teaches stewardship, patience, multiplication, responsibility and service. A trainee who learns fish farming well can feed families, create jobs, support communities and build a sustainable livelihood.

8. Target Trainees
This module is designed for:
1. Unemployed graduates.
2. Secondary school leavers.
3. Orphans and vulnerable young people.
4. Youths interested in agriculture and agribusiness.
5. Trainees interested in self-employment.
6. Trainees who want to learn food production.
7. Community development workers.
8. Trainees who may later work in farms, cooperatives, hatcheries, feed businesses, processing units or agribusiness enterprises.

9. Duration
This departmental module runs within the one-year Academy structure.
Phase One: Classroom and Practical Training
Seven months
This phase covers aquaculture foundation, pond systems, fish species, stocking, feeding, water quality, fish health, harvesting, marketing, record keeping and business planning.
Phase Two: Industrial Attachment
Five months
This phase places trainees in fish farms, hatcheries, aquaculture enterprises, feed businesses, processing units, agricultural projects or cooperative farms for supervised workplace experience.

10. Important Professional Note
This module provides basic to intermediate practical training in fish farming. It does not replace advanced professional training in fisheries science, veterinary medicine, aquaculture engineering, water chemistry, environmental management or business finance.
Trainees must be taught to seek guidance from qualified aquaculture officers, fisheries professionals, veterinary personnel or experienced farm managers when dealing with serious disease outbreaks, high mortality, water contamination, chemical use, large-scale pond construction or major investment decisions.

UNIT 1
Introduction to Fish Farming and Aquaculture
Purpose
To introduce trainees to the meaning, value and opportunities of fish farming.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Define fish farming.

3. Identify reasons people farm fish.
4. Explain how fish farming can reduce unemployment.

6. Understand fish farming as a business and service.
Teaching Content
Fish farming is the controlled raising of fish for food, income, breeding, processing or sale. It is different from catching fish from rivers, lakes or streams. In fish farming, the farmer provides water, feed, care, protection and management.
Fish farming is important because it can:
1. Provide protein for families.
2. Create employment.
3. Generate income.
4. Support rural development.
5. Reduce pressure on wild fish.
6. Encourage agribusiness.
7. Provide opportunities for youth.
8. Support community food security.

Take trainees to the fish pond or tank area and identify:
1. Water source.
2. Pond or tank.
3. Fish feed.
4. Nets.
5. Farm records.
6. Harvesting tools.
7. Waste disposal area.
8. Feed storage area.
Group Activity
Ask trainees to discuss:
How can fish farming help families and communities in Taraba State and Nigeria?

Trainees should explain the difference between fish farming and fishing.

UNIT 3
Common Farmed Fish Species
Purpose
To introduce trainees to common fish species suitable for farming.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Identify common farmed fish.
2. Explain why catfish is popular in Nigeria.
3. Explain why tilapia is commonly farmed.
4. Understand basic differences between fish species.
5. Know that different fish need different management.
6. Avoid mixing fish care methods carelessly.
Teaching Content
Common farmed fish in Nigeria and many African settings include:
1. Catfish.
2. Tilapia.
3. Carp in some areas.
4. Heterobranchus.
5. Clarias species.
6. Hybrid catfish.
Catfish
Catfish is popular because it is hardy, grows well under good management and has strong market demand. It can be raised in earthen ponds, concrete tanks, tarpaulin ponds and plastic tanks.
Tilapia
Tilapia is also widely farmed. It can grow well in ponds, but farmers must understand breeding, stocking and population control because tilapia can reproduce quickly.
Important Teaching Point
The department should focus mainly on fish species that are locally available, affordable, marketable and suitable for the Academy’s water system and climate.
Practical Demonstration

1. Fingerlings.

1. Growth.

UNIT 4
Fish Pond and Tank Systems
Purpose
To teach trainees different fish farming systems and their advantages and disadvantages.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Identify different pond and tank systems.
2. Explain the advantages of earthen ponds.
3. Explain the advantages of concrete tanks.
4. Explain the use of tarpaulin ponds.
5. Understand basic system selection.
6. Choose a system based on water, space, capital and management ability.
Teaching Content
Fish may be raised in different systems.
A. Earthen Pond
An earthen pond is dug into the ground. It can be suitable where land and water are available.
Advantages:
1. Natural environment.
2. Can support plankton growth.
3. Good for larger production.
4. May have lower operating cost after construction.
5. Challenges:
6. Requires suitable soil.
7. Harder to monitor fish.
8. More difficult to harvest completely.
9. May allow predators if poorly managed.
B. Concrete Tank
A concrete tank is built with cement blocks or reinforced concrete.
Advantages:
1. Easier to manage.
2. Easier to clean.
3. Easier to harvest.
4. Suitable for controlled farming.
5. Challenges:
6. Higher construction cost.
7. Water quality must be managed carefully.
8. Heat may affect water if exposed.
C. Tarpaulin Pond
A tarpaulin pond is made with a frame and water-holding liner.
Advantages:
1. Easier to install.
2. Movable in some cases.
3. Good for training and small farms.
4. Lower initial cost than concrete in some settings.
5. Challenges:
6. Can tear.
7. Needs careful handling.
8. May not last as long as concrete.
D. Plastic or Fibre Tank
These are ready-made tanks used for fish culture.
Advantages:
1. Easy to install.
2. Easy to clean.
3. Good for small-scale farming.
4. Useful for beginners.
5. Challenges:
6. Limited size.
7. Cost may be high.
8. Needs reliable water.
Practical Demonstration
Take trainees around available pond or tank systems and discuss:
1. Cost.
2. Durability.
3. Cleaning.
4. Water use.
5. Stocking capacity.
6. Harvesting ease.
Group Activity
Divide trainees into groups. Each group should choose one system and explain why it may be suitable for a beginner.
Assessment
Trainees should list three types of fish farming systems and one advantage of each.
Character Focus
Wisdom in Planning
Trainees should choose systems based on reality, not pride.

Good site selection can determine whether a fish farm succeeds or fails.

4. Good drainage.
5. Accessibility.
6. Security.

UNIT 6
Pond Preparation Before Stocking
Purpose
To teach trainees how to prepare ponds or tanks before introducing fish.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Clean a pond or tank before stocking.
2. Repair leaks or damage.
3. Remove predators and dirt.
4. Fill pond or tank correctly.
5. Prepare water before stocking.
6. Understand why preparation prevents losses.
Teaching Content
Pond preparation depends on the type of system.
A. For Concrete or Tarpaulin Tanks
Steps may include:
1. Wash the tank.
2. Remove dirt.
3. Check for leakage.
4. Rinse properly.
5. Fill with clean water.
6. Allow water to settle where appropriate.
7. Check for harmful smell.
8. Ensure inlet and outlet are working.
9. Protect from predators.
10. Prepare stocking records.
B. For Earthen Ponds
Steps may include:
1. Drain old water where needed.
2. Remove unwanted fish.
3. Remove weeds.
4. Repair pond walls.
5. Clear inlet and outlet.
6. Apply lime where recommended by an experienced professional.
7. Fertilise only when appropriate and under guidance.
8. Fill water to proper level.
9. Allow natural pond condition to stabilise before stocking.
10. Protect from predators.
Practical Demonstration
Prepare one pond or tank as a class demonstration.
Trainees should observe and participate in:
1. Cleaning.
2. Checking water inlet.
3. Checking outlet.
4. Inspecting for leakage.
5. Recording preparation date.
6. Preparing stocking sheet.
Group Activity
Groups should prepare a pond preparation checklist for concrete, tarpaulin or earthen systems.
Assessment
Trainees should explain why fish should not be stocked into an unprepared pond.
Character Focus
Preparation
Trainees should learn that good results begin before the fish arrive.

UNIT 7
Water Quality Management
Purpose
To train trainees to observe, protect and manage water quality.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Explain why water quality is important.
2. Identify signs of poor water.
3. Observe water colour, smell and behaviour.
4. Understand the importance of oxygen.
5. Understand the effect of dirty water.
6. Know when to report water problems.
Teaching Content
Water is the living environment of fish. Poor water can cause stress, poor feeding, disease or death.
Important water factors include:
1. Oxygen.
2. Temperature.
3. pH.
4. Ammonia.
5. Cleanliness.
6. Water colour.
7. Smell.
8. Waste build-up.
9. Water movement.
10. Overcrowding.
11. Decomposing feed.
12. Dead fish.
Signs of Water Problems
Trainees should watch for:
1. Fish gasping at the surface.
2. Fish refusing feed.
3. Bad smell.
4. Very dirty water.
5. Sudden change in water colour.
6. Dead fish.
7. Weak swimming.
8. Foam or oily surface.
9. Excess waste.
10. Fish gathering near water inlet.
Basic Water Management Practices
1. Avoid overfeeding.
2. Remove uneaten feed where possible.
3. Change water when necessary.
4. Avoid overcrowding.
5. Remove dead fish immediately.
6. Keep inlet and outlet clean.
7. Protect pond from contamination.
8. Observe fish daily.
9. Record water changes.
10. Seek expert advice for serious problems.
Practical Demonstration
Show trainees how to observe and record:
1. Water colour.
2. Water smell.
3. Fish behaviour.
4. Feeding response.
5. Water level.
6. Waste accumulation.
7. Where available, demonstrate use of:
8. pH strip.
9. Thermometer.
10. Dissolved oxygen meter.
11. Ammonia test kit.
Group Activity
Each group should inspect one pond or tank and complete a water observation sheet.
Assessment
Trainees should list five signs that water quality may be poor.
Character Focus
Daily Observation
Trainees should learn to check the pond before problems become losses.

UNIT 8
Fish Seed Selection and Stocking
Purpose
To teach trainees how to select healthy fish seed and stock ponds correctly.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Explain fingerlings and juveniles.
2. Identify signs of healthy fish seed.
3. Understand the danger of poor-quality seed.
4. Acclimatise fish before stocking.
5. Stock fish gently.
6. Record stocking information.
Teaching Content
Fish seed refers to young fish used to start production.
Common terms:
1. Fry.
2. Fingerlings.
3. Juveniles.
4. Post-juveniles.
5. Grow-out fish.
Signs of Good Fish Seed
Good fish seed should:
1. Be active.
2. Swim well.
3. Have uniform size as much as possible.
4. Have no wounds.
5. Show no obvious deformity.
6. Come from a trusted hatchery.
7. Be transported carefully.
8. Respond well when released.
Stocking Principles
Before stocking:
1. Prepare the pond.
2. Confirm water is suitable.
3. Count fish.
4. Record source.
5. Record size.
6. Record date.
7. Avoid stocking during extreme heat.
8. Acclimatise fish gradually.
9. Release gently.
10. Observe after stocking.
Acclimatisation
Acclimatisation helps fish adjust to new water conditions. Sudden transfer can stress or kill fish.
Simple approach:
1. Float transport bag or container where possible.
2. Allow temperature adjustment.
3. Gradually mix small amounts of pond water.
4. Release fish gently.
5. Observe behaviour.
Practical Demonstration
Demonstrate proper stocking using a small number of fish.
Group Activity
Trainees should complete a stocking record sheet.
Assessment
Trainees should explain why fish should not be poured roughly into a pond.
Character Focus
Gentleness and Responsibility
Trainees should handle living creatures with care.

UNIT 9
Feeding and Feed Management
Purpose
To train trainees on proper feeding, feed observation, feed storage and waste prevention.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Explain why feeding is important.
2. Identify different feed sizes.
3. Feed fish according to size and stage.
4. Observe feeding response.
5. Avoid overfeeding and underfeeding.
6. Store feed properly.
7. Keep feeding records.
Teaching Content
Feed is one of the biggest costs in fish farming. Poor feeding can destroy profit.
Fish feeding must consider:
1. Fish size.
2. Fish age.
3. Water quality.
4. Weather.
5. Feeding response.
6. Feed quality.
7. Stocking density.
8. Farm goal.
9. Budget.
10. Growth stage.
Overfeeding
Overfeeding can lead to:
1. Wasted feed.
2. Dirty water.
3. Bad smell.
4. Poor oxygen.
5. Disease risk.
6. Higher cost.
7. Loss of profit.
Underfeeding
Underfeeding can lead to:
1. Slow growth.
2. Unequal sizes.
3. Cannibalism in catfish.
4. Weak fish.
5. Longer production time.
6. Poor harvest.
Feed Storage
Feed should be stored:
1. In a dry place.
2. Away from water.
3. Away from rodents.
4. Away from direct sunlight.
5. Off the floor where possible.
6. In closed bags or containers.
7. According to purchase date.
8. Not beyond safe storage period.
Feeding Observation
During feeding, trainees should observe:
1. How quickly fish come to feed.
2. Whether fish are weak.
3. Whether some fish are not feeding.
4. Whether feed remains uneaten.
5. Whether water becomes dirty.
6. Whether large fish dominate small fish.
Practical Demonstration
Demonstrate feeding by:
1. Measuring feed.
2. Feeding gradually.
3. Observing fish response.
4. Stopping when fish response reduces.
5. Recording quantity used.
Group Activity
Each group should prepare a simple feeding chart.
Assessment
Trainees should explain the dangers of overfeeding and underfeeding.
Character Focus
Discipline and Waste Prevention
Trainees should not waste feed because feed is money.

UNIT 10
Fish Growth Monitoring, Sorting and Grading
Purpose
To teach trainees how to monitor fish growth and manage size differences.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Understand fish growth monitoring.
2. Weigh sample fish.
3. Record growth.
4. Identify uneven growth.
5. Explain sorting and grading.
6. Handle fish safely during grading.
Teaching Content
Fish do not always grow at the same rate. Some grow faster than others. In catfish farming, bigger fish may injure or eat smaller ones if size differences become too wide.
Growth monitoring helps the farmer know:
1. Whether fish are growing.
2. Whether feeding is adequate.
3. Whether water quality is good.
4. Whether grading is needed.
5. Whether harvest time is near.
6. Whether there is a problem.
Sorting and Grading
Sorting means separating fish according to size.
Benefits:
1. Reduces cannibalism.
2. Promotes uniform growth.
3. Improves feeding.
4. Helps farm planning.
5. Reduces stress among smaller fish.
Practical Demonstration
Demonstrate how to:
1. Catch sample fish gently.
2. Weigh sample fish.
3. Record weight.
4. Compare sizes.
5. Separate fish into size groups.
6. Return fish carefully.
Group Activity
Trainees should fill a sample growth monitoring chart.
Assessment
Trainees should explain why grading is important in catfish farming.
Character Focus
Careful Handling
Trainees should handle fish calmly and avoid unnecessary injury.

UNIT 11
Fish Health, Disease Prevention and Biosecurity
Purpose
To teach trainees basic fish health observation, disease prevention and farm biosecurity.
Learning Objectives
By the end of this unit, trainees should be able to:
1. Identify signs of healthy fish.
2. Identify warning signs of sick fish.
3. Understand common causes of disease.
4. Practise basic biosecurity.
5. Remove dead fish safely.
6. Know when to seek expert help.
Teaching Content
Healthy fish are usually active, feed well and swim normally.
Warning signs include:
1. Loss of appetite.
2. Weak swimming.
3. Gasping.
4. Wounds.
5. Red patches.
6. Swollen belly.
7. Unusual colour.
8. Fish rubbing against surfaces.
9. Sudden death.
10. Fish gathering in one area.
11. Bad water smell.
12. Many fish becoming weak at once.
Common Causes of Fish Health Problems
1. Poor water quality.
2. Overcrowding.
3. Bad feed.
4. Overfeeding.
5. Dirty pond.
6. Stress during handling.
7. Poor-quality fingerlings.
8. Contaminated water.
9. Predators.
10. Disease introduction from outside farms.
Biosecurity
Biosecurity means practices that reduce the entry and spread of disease.
Basic biosecurity practices include:
1. Buy fish seed from trusted sources.
2. Avoid bringing dirty equipment from other farms.
3. Clean nets and tools.
4. Remove dead fish immediately.
5. Do not throw dead fish near ponds.
